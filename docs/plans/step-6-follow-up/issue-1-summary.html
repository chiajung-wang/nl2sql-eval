Schema-token-budget retrieval crossover

Author: Chia-Jung Wang (@chiajung-wang) Issue: #75 Branch: step-6-follow-up/issue-75-budget-experiment → main

1 Overview

The honest other half of Step 6: schema-RAG can't win on the "it doesn't fit" axis (BIRD's biggest schema is ~1.8K tokens), so measure where it does pay — under a configured schema-token budget.

Step 6 reported retrieval lift −0.125: when the whole schema already fits the context, RAG can only drop a needed table and lose. This experiment imposes a schema-token budget (a cost/latency policy a real system sets — not the model's context limit) and asks the sharper question: under that budget, is it better to truncate the schema (declaration order) or to retrieve the relevant tables? The frozen Step-6 slice runs both ways across a budget sweep.

The headline finding is a measurement-discipline one: a first draft claimed RAG "beats truncation at every budget," but the independent review forced the truth out — most of that gap is sampling noise, and the robust signal is recall.

Why 4096t is the control, not a win. The largest schema in the slice (european_football_2) renders to 3820 tokens, so at 4096t every schema fits and the two modes select identical tables (selection divergence 0 — 0/40 questions differ). Generation runs at non-zero temperature, so two independent runs on the same prompt still differ by ~0.050 (2/40). That is the sampling-noise floor. Read the tight-budget gaps against it: the peak +0.100 @512t is ~2× noise; the rest sit within ~1 floor — so on this 40-question slice the pass@1 advantage is suggestive, not conclusive.

Recall is monotone and unambiguous: more budget lets retrieval cover more of the gold tables, while truncation cuts blindly. The pass@1 effect is real but small against sampling noise — so recall, not pass@1, carries the claim.
